## Answer

The code supports the central mechanism in **(a)**: an orbit that crosses the window contributes a present-phase term from its current region and an orbit-average term from every region it visits. Those terms couple the regional curves through the likelihood. Each cluster contributes `ln C(now) − ln <C(t)>` ([cv.py:199–207](gc_completeness/cv.py:199), [cv.py:150–157](gc_completeness/cv.py:150)); the region label is assigned separately at every orbit sample ([run_cv.py:173–177](scripts/run_cv.py:173), [run_cv.py:613–616](scripts/run_cv.py:613)). So a cluster seen outside whose orbit enters the window can affect both curves. The supplied counts—86 crossing orbits and only 8 never entering the window—make that coupling plausible, though they do not measure how much of the outer slope it explains ([real_cv_summary.json:186–189](outputs/cv/real_cv_summary.json:186)).

**(b), (c), and (d) are plausible, but the current results do not distinguish them.** The outer region is the entire sky outside the window, not a dust-free control sample ([run_cv.py:173–177](scripts/run_cv.py:173)). The likelihood evaluates every orbit sample, so dusty samples outside can matter even if they are a minority. In the no-dust fit, variation in dust loss that is omitted from `V_t` can be attributed to the fitted completeness curves; the apparent magnitude calculation includes extinction only when an `A_V` value is supplied ([cv.py:18–27](gc_completeness/cv.py:18), [run_cv.py:93–98](scripts/run_cv.py:93)). But the fraction of dusty samples alone cannot establish their influence on the outer faint-end slope.

Hypothesis **(c)** needs a qualification: the two-region fit cannot represent arbitrary dust variation within either region, but “leakage” is mediated by the crossing-orbit likelihood terms. In the separate-spline model, region 0 is anchored and region 1 has its own unanchored spline ([cv.py:219–246](gc_completeness/cv.py:219)); there is no shared spline shape in that model. The constant-contrast alternative does share a spline ([cv.py:257–286](gc_completeness/cv.py:257)). Thus a shared anchor is not, by itself, an explanation for the separate-spline result.

Hypothesis **(d)** is especially worth checking. Regional nodes are set from present-day magnitudes of clusters currently in each region ([cv.py:207–216](gc_completeness/cv.py:207)), while the fit uses magnitudes across the orbits. The spline extrapolates beyond its last node using the last segment’s slope, with a non-increasing tail ([cv.py:183–204](gc_completeness/cv.py:183), [cv.py:249–254](gc_completeness/cv.py:249)). The saved summary gives the outer curve at \(V=14\), but does not save the regional node positions; that summary alone cannot tell whether this point is interpolated or extrapolated ([real_cv_summary.json:690–717](outputs/cv/real_cv_summary.json:690), [real_cv_summary.json:719–746](outputs/cv/real_cv_summary.json:719)).

**(e) cannot be ruled out.** The phase likelihood tests whether observed phases are consistent with the fitted selection along the adopted orbits; it does not identify the physical cause of phase-dependent selection ([phaselike.py:1–12](gc_completeness/phaselike.py:1)). Dust maps could absorb another effect correlated with dusty sightlines. The remaining outer PIT discrepancy under the map fits is a reason not to call the maps a complete explanation: the saved map-high regional fits have outer PIT \(p\) values around 0.013–0.016 ([real_cv_summary_map_high.json:128–137](outputs/cv/real_cv_summary_map_high.json:128), [real_cv_summary_map_high.json:612–618](outputs/cv/real_cv_summary_map_high.json:612)). Those values are diagnostics, not proof of a specific alternative cause.

The 77-weight reduction is therefore unsurprising: the regional model only partially changes the fitted correction. On the supplied totals it reduces the no-dust halo weight from 453 to 376, while the no-dust versus map single-curve difference is 318. The saved regional summaries support the broader pattern—no-dust separate curves improve AIC by about 4.2, while map-high separate curves are essentially tied with the single curve—but do not identify why the no-dust outer curve remains steep ([real_cv_summary.json:674–680](outputs/cv/real_cv_summary.json:674), [real_cv_summary_map_high.json:612–618](outputs/cv/real_cv_summary_map_high.json:612)). At \(V=14\), the saved separate outer curves are about 0.075 without dust and 0.289 with map-high extinction ([real_cv_summary.json:737–741](outputs/cv/real_cv_summary.json:737), [real_cv_summary_map_high.json:671–675](outputs/cv/real_cv_summary_map_high.json:671)).

## Cheap diagnostics

1. **Decompose the no-dust outer-slope score by cluster.** At the fitted parameters, calculate each cluster’s likelihood derivative with respect to the outer faint-end slope, then group contributions by present region, crossing fraction, and orbit-sample \(A_V\). The likelihood’s numerator and orbit-average form supplies this decomposition ([cv.py:150–157](gc_completeness/cv.py:150)). It directly tests whether crossing orbits or dusty off-window samples drive the slope. The saved CSV has present-day weights and \(A_V\), but the run saves no orbit-sample `V_t` or `reg_t` arrays; reconstruct these from the orbit and dust caches or regenerate them ([run_cv.py:658–665](scripts/run_cv.py:658)).

2. **Fit a three-region model** separating the inner window, low-latitude sky outside it, and the remaining sky. Compare the outer slope and halo weights with the two-region fit. Check that each region has enough present clusters and that orbit crossings connect the free regional levels; the current regional fitter requires at least four clusters per region ([cv.py:207–216](gc_completeness/cv.py:207)). An \(A_V>1\) versus \(A_V\le1\) partition is a useful complementary sensitivity check, but it is an extinction-defined partition, not an independent validation of the maps.

3. **Measure tail leverage.** Refit after changing the outer-region node count or excluding the faintest present-day outer clusters, and report whether \(C_{\rm out}(14)\) is interpolated or tail-extrapolated in each fit. The present nodes come from clusters now in the region, and the extrapolated tail inherits the last-segment slope ([cv.py:207–216](gc_completeness/cv.py:207), [cv.py:198–204](gc_completeness/cv.py:198)).

4. **Run leave-one-out influence fits for halo weights.** A cluster’s saved weight is not its influence on the fitted curve. Delete each cluster in turn, refit, and track the outer slope and summed halo weights. The current bootstrap resamples clusters and refits, but it is not a leave-one-out influence analysis ([run_cv.py:211–219](scripts/run_cv.py:211)).

Excluding all crossers is a useful sensitivity test only with care: removing them also removes much of the information that connects the regional levels. A near-flat or unstable inner offset in that fit would reflect lost identifiability, not evidence that crossing orbits had no influence ([cv.py:179–181](gc_completeness/cv.py:179), [cv.py:249–254](gc_completeness/cv.py:249)).

## What the write-up should say

Describe the two-region result as a **sensitivity analysis of directional misspecification**, not as a replacement for mapped extinction. The present write-up says the maps “already carry the direction dependence” ([gc_completeness.tex:903–906](writeup/gc_completeness.tex:903)); I would qualify that: *With the adopted maps, the data do not prefer additional flexibility for this particular two-region split. The split does not explain the no-dust halo-weight excess, and the remaining outer phase diagnostic leaves model mismatch possible.* The map-high regional model changes the total from 432 to 485, while its AIC is essentially tied with the single map fit ([gc_completeness.tex:903–912](writeup/gc_completeness.tex:903); [real_cv_summary_map_high.json:612–618](outputs/cv/real_cv_summary_map_high.json:612)). Neither result establishes that the map-based correction is physically right.

## Verified versus assumed

**Verified in code:** the regional label is assigned per orbit sample; the likelihood couples present-region terms to all regions visited along the orbit; separate curves use region-specific present-day nodes; and the faint tail extrapolates with a constrained slope ([run_cv.py:173–177](scripts/run_cv.py:173), [cv.py:150–157](gc_completeness/cv.py:150), [cv.py:207–246](gc_completeness/cv.py:207), [cv.py:198–204](gc_completeness/cv.py:198)). The output summaries show the stated AIC and curve values cited above.

**Assumed from the question packet:** the counts of dusty orbit samples, the halo-weight sums, and the quoted \(C_{\rm out}(V\!\sim\!14)\) summaries. The summary files support the curve comparison, but I did not independently recompute the sample fractions or halo sums from the underlying catalogue and orbit arrays.

## Strongest argument against this answer

The near-zero map-high AIC difference and broadly similar map-high regional weights could mean the dust correction has already removed the relevant spatial selection effect. In that case, the residual outer PIT result could be a noisy diagnostic, while the no-dust outer slope is simply the likelihood’s response to omitted extinction. The available fits alone cannot distinguish that simpler explanation from maps absorbing another correlated phase-selection effect.
